## Overview

A single-player deduction roguelike. You are the entire abuse desk at a mid-size registrar. Each run is one eight-hour shift: reports come in, you pivot through infrastructure artifacts to find the rest of the campaign, and you submit a takedown list before the shift ends. Scored on coverage minus collateral damage.

## Problem

Investigation games are almost all *narrative* — read documents, pick the murderer. Real threat-intel pivoting is a graph search under a time budget against an adversary who reuses things until you punish them for it, and that loop has never been made into a game. Meanwhile, real analysts have a specific tragedy nobody has dramatized: the detection rules you write to survive today's volume are what nuke an innocent bakery next month.

## How it works

1. Shift opens with 2–4 reported domains and 480 minutes.
2. **Pivot** on any artifact: WHOIS registrant email (5 min), passive DNS for an IP (20), Certificate Transparency SAN search (15), favicon hash (10), HTML template hash (25), name server tenancy (10). Each reveals neighboring nodes in a hidden graph.
3. The campaign generator gave this operator a hidden **habit set** — e.g. *reuses favicon, rotates registrar weekly, always 40+ SANs per cert*. Finding the habit is finding the campaign. Two or three habits, drawn from a pool of ~12.
4. Deliberately seeded near-misses: a legit bakery on the same shared IP, a real bank whose cert also has 40 SANs. Take those down and you get an email from a furious human, and lose more points than the domain was worth.
5. Submit list → score recall × precision, weighted by collateral severity.
6. **Meta-progression, the twist:** between shifts you write one *rule* in a tiny DSL (`cert.san_count > 40 AND ns.tenant == "cheapdns" -> flag`). Rules run automatically at the start of every future shift, saving real minutes — and auto-flagging things you now have to audit. Your rule list is a growing pile of your past self's overconfidence. You may retire rules, but only one per shift.
7. The generator adapts lightly: each new campaign's habits are sampled with a bias *away* from whatever your active rules match, so your best rule reliably decays into a false-positive machine.

## Technical approach

- **Stack:** TypeScript + Svelte, all client-side, no backend, no real data (which is also the ethics answer — nothing here touches live infrastructure).
- **Generation:** seeded PRNG (`mulberry32`) so runs are shareable by seed. Campaign = a graph built by sampling 2–3 habits, then instantiating 15–40 domains: names from a dictionary + typo-squat + homoglyph + keyword-prepend model (`secure-<brand>-login.<tld>`), certs with plausible SAN sets and issuer distribution, IPs assigned from generated ASN blocks with realistic tenancy skew.
- **Legit population:** ~200 benign entities generated by the *same* pipeline with different parameters — this is what makes collateral damage feel earned rather than gotcha.
- **Rule DSL:** a 30-line Pratt parser to an AST, evaluated against artifact objects. Show matched-count previews when authoring.
- **Hard part:** making procedural infrastructure feel *real* enough that the pivots teach something true. If cert SANs are random noise, the game is Minesweeper with jargon. Fidelity comes from encoding actual reuse statistics (shared hosting density, registrar concentration, cert issuer share).

## v1 scope

- One shift, one campaign, three pivot types (WHOIS, passive DNS, CT)
- 4 habits in the pool
- Submit list, get a score screen naming which habit you missed
- No rules, no meta-progression, no adaptation

## Out of scope

Real data ingest, multiplayer, narrative characters, a research tree, mobile.

## Risks & unknowns

- Vocabulary wall: non-security players may bounce in 90 seconds without a strong first-shift tutorial.
- The rule DSL may be too fiddly to be fun; fallback is picking from pre-written rule cards.
- Adaptive generator can feel unfair rather than sly if the bias is too strong.

## Done means

A playtester with no security background finishes their third shift, correctly names the operator's habit out loud before submitting, and groans when their own rule flags the bakery.
