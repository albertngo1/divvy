## Overview
A compliance-intelligence service for small and mid-size brands importing radio-bearing consumer electronics — earbuds, trackers, LED controllers, dashcams. They pay $30–60k for FCC Part 15 testing and certification; a marketplace competitor pays nothing, prints a borrowed FCC ID on the label, and undercuts them by 40%. Grantee finds those listings and packages the proof.

## Problem
Everything needed is public — and nobody assembles it. FCC equipment-authorization records are free bulk downloads. Listings are free to view. Grant PDFs include internal photos and the exact tested model number. But cross-referencing an image of a label against a grant database at scale is a data-engineering job the compliance manager at a 12-person audio brand will never do. That gap is the whole arbitrage: cheap for us, unreachable for them, and worth real money because the outcome is a competitor's listing coming down.

## How it works
Customer sets a watch: category, keyword set, price ceiling. Nightly we ingest listings, OCR every listing image (label shots, box back panels, PDF manuals), and extract candidate FCC IDs, model numbers, and grantee names. Then we join against the FCC OET Equipment Authorization System.

Flags we raise, each with a citation to a primary record:
- Intentional radiator with no FCC ID anywhere in listing or manual
- FCC ID resolves to a grant whose **product type or frequency band doesn't match** the listing (the classic borrowed-ID pattern: a Bluetooth speaker's grant on a drone)
- Grantee code registered to an unrelated company
- Grant date after the listing's first-seen date
- Declaration-of-Conformity language used on a device that legally requires certification

Cross-references: CPSC SaferProducts recall data, DOE CCMS certification for external power supplies, UN38.3 lithium claims.

Output is a signed PDF packet: timestamped screenshots with content hashes, the extracted label crop, the actual FCC grant PDF, and a diff table — pre-formatted for Amazon's Report a Product Violation flow and for an FCC complaint.

## Technical approach
Python + Postgres. Weekly ingest of the FCC pipe-delimited bulk dumps (PUBACC_GRANT, PUBACC_APPLICATION, PUBACC_GRANTEE) into normalized tables; grantee-code prefix index. Listing acquisition via official APIs where they exist (eBay Browse, Amazon PA-API for the customer's own catalog) and licensed SERP data elsewhere — ToS-clean by construction, because the customer can also just upload photos of a sample they bought. Label OCR: PaddleOCR for the crop plus a regex/edit-distance grammar over the FCC ID format (3- or 5-char grantee code + product code), with a small classifier to locate label regions in cluttered marketing photos. Fuzzy company-name resolution via normalized trigram similarity. Evidence packets rendered with WeasyPrint, hash-chained daily.

Hard part: the join. Most listings never show a label. Recall is the business risk, not precision — so the product is explicitly "flags we can prove," plus a sample-teardown upload path for the rest.

## v1 scope
- One category (Bluetooth audio), one marketplace
- Manual watch setup, no self-serve signup
- Three flag types: missing ID, product-type mismatch, grantee mismatch
- PDF packet generated by hand-triggered script

## Out of scope
EU CE/UKCA, automated filing, public naming-and-shaming, any customer-facing dashboard.

## Risks & unknowns
A wrong flag is a defamation exposure — mitigation is never publishing, only delivering to the customer, and citing the primary record on every line. Marketplace enforcement may ignore packets. Scraping constraints are real and shape acquisition.

## Done means
On 100 hand-adjudicated listings in one category, ≥85% precision on "no valid matching grant," and one delivered packet results in an actual marketplace takedown.
